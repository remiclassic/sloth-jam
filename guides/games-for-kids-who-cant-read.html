Games for kids who can’t read yet (ages 5–6)

A parent guide · October 2026

At five or six, most children learn best by watching and doing. Reading is still new. Good games for this age show a goal with images, use a tiny number of inputs and give a quick turn. The ideas below help you set up play that feels fair and calm.

Choose visual, not verbal

  • Teach by example. When you start a round, say what you do and show it. A child who cannot read can still copy a motion.
  • Use symbols and numbers. A heart, a star, a fruit and a score that ticks up are easy to learn without words.
  • Avoid busy screens. Fewer buttons and big targets win. If a game has a long rule text, skip it for now.

Keep turns short

A one minute turn keeps a child engaged, and it reduces fights about sharing. In Sloth Jam every party game lasts one minute and a match is three rounds, so the device comes back often and each round feels small.

Pick simple inputs

  • One stick and one button. This is the sweet spot for small hands. Push to move, tap to act, hold to do a bigger action.
  • No hidden combos. Avoid games that expect many button presses in a pattern. Save that for later years.
  • Room to watch. Make sure a parent can sit beside the child to model a move during the first turns.

Show progress without pressure

  • Stars and stickers. Rewards that do not require reading are easy to celebrate. A book of goals with pictures works well.
  • No pay to win. For young kids, avoid loot boxes and random paid rewards. Sloth Jam does not use loot boxes, and it is free while in early access.
  • Let them replay. Repeat a favorite game and cheer the small improvements. A gentle ghost to chase can help too, if it feels fun.

Ideas for mixed ages

  • Alternate young and old. Seat a grandparent or an older sibling between two young kids. Let the youngest pick a game once.
  • Use a team round to reset. A fully cooperative game helps when someone feels left out. In Sloth Jam there is Lantern Parade where nobody loses and all share the stars.
  • Match length. Two or three short matches is a full session. End early if attention fades, then go color and come back later.

Try these kinds of games

  • Big movement, simple goal. Catch fruit in a basket, keep a hat on your head, jump over small rolling things.
  • Memory pairs. Turn over cards to find a match. Pictures beat words.
  • Gentle racing. A kart race where everyone earns points and nobody is stuck in last for long.
